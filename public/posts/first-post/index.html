Overlooked: The CBC-R attack

August 23, 2026
#crypto

Introduction

Back in November of 2023 I found out about a 13 year old basic padding oracle attack that was somehow completely new to me. A CBC padding oracle technique which I had not encountered before in a paper, a ctf, or cryptohack. Thinking it was surely common knowledge I went to find a library that would do the work for me (yes, I know, I’m lazy) and found to my amazement none of the libraries I checked (tbf: all python) even mentioned it.

  • Padding-oracle attacks are widely taught as a way to decrypt CBC ciphertext without the key.
  • CBC-R, introduced by Rizzo and Duong in 2010, shows that the same oracle can also encrypt arbitrary attacker-chosen plaintext.
  • Despite this stronger capability, CBC-R rarely appears in educational material, implementations or CTF challenges.
  • Article goal: explain the overlooked technique, demonstrate it and show why it changes the impact of a padding oracle.

The Familiar Padding Oracle

  • Briefly explain CBC decryption:

    Pi=DK(Ci)⊕Ci−1 P_i = D_K(C_i) \oplus C_{i-1} Pi​=DK​(Ci​)⊕Ci−1​

  • Define the intermediate value (I_i = D_K(C_i)).

  • Show how a padding oracle lets the attacker recover (I_i) byte by byte.

  • Standard presentations then use it to recover existing plaintext:

    Pi=Ii⊕Ci−1 P_i = I_i \oplus C_{i-1} Pi​=Ii​⊕Ci−1​

Turning Decryption into Encryption

  • The recovered intermediate value can instead be combined with a chosen plaintext block (P_i^*):

    Ci−1∗=Ii⊕Pi∗ C_{i-1}^* = I_i \oplus P_i^* Ci−1∗​=Ii​⊕Pi∗​

  • The resulting ciphertext decrypts to the attacker’s chosen block without revealing the key.

  • This is more powerful than ordinary CBC bit flipping because it can construct entirely new messages rather than modifying known plaintext.

Constructing a Message Backwards

  • Pad and split the chosen plaintext into blocks.
  • Choose a random final ciphertext block.
  • Use the oracle to recover its intermediate value.
  • Calculate the preceding block required to produce the chosen final plaintext block.
  • Repeat backwards until the first block, with the final calculated value becoming the IV.
  • Include a short implementation or CTF-style demonstration.

Why CBC-R Matters

  • It expands the impact of a padding oracle from plaintext disclosure to ciphertext forgery.

  • Potential uses include:

    • Forging encrypted cookies or application state.
    • Creating tokens with attacker-selected roles or identities.
    • Supplying chosen structured data to downstream parsers.
    • Delivering malicious serialized objects, as in padding-oracle/deserialisation exploit chains.

  • The attacker never recovers the encryption key; they exploit the application’s mistaken trust in unauthenticated CBC ciphertext.

Why Is It Overlooked?

  • Most explanations stop once plaintext recovery succeeds.
  • CBC bit flipping is more commonly taught as CBC’s forgery attack.
  • CBC-R requires working backwards across multiple blocks, making it slightly less intuitive to demonstrate.
  • Padding oracles are consequently often described only as confidentiality vulnerabilities.
  • This absence is especially noticeable in CTF challenges, which commonly ask players to decrypt a secret but rarely ask them to forge a chosen encrypted payload.

Mitigation and Conclusion

  • Use authenticated encryption such as AES-GCM or ChaCha20-Poly1305.
  • For legacy CBC, authenticate ciphertext with encrypt-then-MAC before decrypting it.
  • Constant-time padding validation removes one possible oracle but does not provide authenticity.
  • Closing point: a padding oracle is not merely an accidental decryption service; through CBC-R, it can become an encryption service too.
